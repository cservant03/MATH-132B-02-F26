    Objectives

    1. Distinguish a population proportion from a sample proportion.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describe a sampling distribution and explain why sample proportions vary.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Calculate and interpret the mean and standard error of a sample proportion.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Check the conditions for using a normal model for sample proportions.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Use a normal model and R to find probabilities involving sample proportions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    A researcher may want to estimate the proportion of seeds that germinate, wetlands with an invasive plant, or patients who respond to a treatment. Usually, the researcher observes a sample rather than the entire population. Different random samples can produce different estimates. How much variation should we expect, and when would a sample result be surprising?
    🔗
    These notes connect the binomial and normal distributions to questions about proportions. All rates, populations, and data in the examples are hypothetical instructional settings.

    Remark 117. Connection to the Binomial Distribution.

    If \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)\) counts the successes, then \(\hat{p}=X/n\text{.}\) Dividing a random variable by \(n\) divides both its mean and its standard deviation by \(n\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \mu_{\hat{p}}=\frac{np}{n}=p,\\ \sigma_{\hat{p}}=\frac{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}{n}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    These formulas hold under the independent binomial model even when the sampling distribution is not approximately normal.

    Subsection When Is a Normal Model Appropriate?

    Before using a normal curve to calculate probabilities about \(\hat{p}\text{,}\) check the sampling method, independence, and expected counts.
    🔗
    1. Random sampling: The sample should be selected randomly from the population of interest, or the problem should specify an appropriate independent chance model.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Independent observations: One outcome should not determine or strongly influence another. For a simple random sample taken without replacement from a population of size \(N\text{,}\) check the 10% condition:
      🔗
      \begin{gather*} n\leq0.10N. \end{gather*}
      🔗
      This makes the dependence from sampling without replacement small enough to use the usual standard error approximately. A small sampling fraction alone does not resolve dependence caused by a clustered sampling design.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Enough expected successes and failures: Check both parts of the success-failure condition:
      🔗
      \begin{gather*} np\geq10\qquad\text{and}\qquad n(1-p)\geq10. \end{gather*}
      🔗
      These are expected counts under the assumed population proportion, not the observed counts in a particular sample.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 121. Normal Approximation for a Sample Proportion.

    When the conditions are satisfied, the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) is approximately normal, with mean \(p\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\text{.}\) We write
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \hat{p}\sim N\left(p,\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\right). \end{gather*}
    🔗
    As in our normal distribution notes, the first parameter is the mean and the second parameter is the standard deviation. This large-sample result is often called the central limit theorem for sample proportions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Probabilities Involving Sample Proportions

    Definition 126. Standardized Sample Proportion.

    To measure how far an observed sample proportion is from the assumed population proportion, calculate
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} z=\frac{\hat{p}-p}{\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    The result is the number of standard errors above or below \(p\text{.}\) When the normal approximation is appropriate, areas under the standard normal curve approximate sampling probabilities.
    🔗
    🔗
