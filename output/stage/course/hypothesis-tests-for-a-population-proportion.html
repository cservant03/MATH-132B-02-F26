    Subsection Introducing Hypothesis Testing

    A confidence interval asks, “Which values of the population proportion are plausible?” A hypothesis test begins with a particular claim and asks, “Do the sample data provide convincing evidence against this claim?” Both questions depend on the sampling variability of \(\hat{p}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose opponents of a proposed dam claim that more than half of the residents oppose construction. A survey finds that 270 of 500 residents, or 54%, are opposed. The sample proportion is above 50%. But samples vary: could a result this high reasonably occur if the population were evenly divided? A hypothesis test gives us a way to assess that evidence.
    🔗

    Definition 156 Hypothesis Test.

    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 157 Null and Alternative Hypotheses.

    🔗
    🔗
    Research question Alternative Test
    Is the proportion lower than \(p_0\text{?}\) \(H_a:p\lt p_0\) Left-tailed
    Is the proportion higher than \(p_0\text{?}\) \(H_a:p\gt p_0\) Right-tailed
    Is the proportion different from \(p_0\text{?}\) \(H_a:p\ne p_0\) Two-tailed

    Subsection P-values and Decisions

    For the dam survey, imagine repeatedly sampling 500 residents from a population in which exactly half oppose the dam. Some samples would have more than half opposed, and some would have fewer. We measure evidence by asking how often this null model would produce a result at least as far in the direction of the alternative as the observed result.
    🔗

    Definition 159 P-Value.

    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 160 Significance Level.

    🔗
    🔗
    Comparison Decision about \(H_0\) Decision about \(H_a\)
    \(\text{p-value}\leq\alpha\) Reject \(H_0\text{.}\) Support \(H_a\text{.}\)
    \(\text{p-value}\gt\alpha\) Fail to reject \(H_0\text{.}\) Fail to support \(H_a\text{.}\)
    A result meeting the rejection rule is statistically significant at the chosen level. Failing to reject does not establish that \(H_0\) is true. The sample may simply be too small to detect a departure from it.
    🔗

    Example 161 What Would a P-Value of 0.037 Tell Us?

    Suppose a right-tailed test of \(H_0:p=0.50\) for the dam survey gives a p-value of approximately 0.037. Explain its meaning and give the decisions at \(\alpha=0.10\) and \(\alpha=0.01\text{.}\)

    🔗

    Process 162 Hypothesis Test.

    1. Identify the claim and hypotheses. Define \(p\text{,}\) write \(H_0\) and \(H_a\text{,}\) and identify the direction of the test.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Choose the significance level and draw a picture. Mark the appropriate rejection tail or tails. Check the sampling, independence, and count conditions.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Calculate the test statistic. Measure how far the sample result is from the null value in null standard errors.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Make a decision. Compare the test statistic with the critical value or compare the p-value with \(\alpha\text{.}\) Both methods give the same decision when they use the same test.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Write a conclusion in context. State whether there is sufficient evidence for the claim, identifying the population and characteristic.
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection The One-Proportion Z-Test

    Definition 163 Requirements.

    1. The response has two categories, and success is clearly defined.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The observations come from an appropriate random sample or independent binomial model with a common success probability.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Observations are independent or approximately independent. For simple random sampling without replacement, use the guideline \(n\leq0.10N\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The null model has at least 10 expected successes and 10 expected failures:
      🔗
      \begin{gather*} np_0\geq10\qquad\text{and}\qquad n(1-p_0)\geq10. \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    These are expected counts calculated from \(p_0\text{,}\) not the observed counts \(x\) and \(n-x\) used for the Wald confidence interval.
    🔗
    🔗

    Formula 164 One-Proportion Z-Test.

    The observed sample proportion and null standard error are
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \hat{p}=\frac{x}{n},\qquad SE_0=\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    The test statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} z=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{SE_0}=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Alternative P-value R command
    \(p\lt p_0\) \(P(Z\leq z)\) pnorm(z, 0, 1)
    \(p\gt p_0\) \(P(Z\geq z)\) pnorm(z, 0, 1, lower.tail = FALSE)
    \(p\ne p_0\) \(2P(Z\geq |z|)\) 2 * pnorm(abs(z), 0, 1, lower.tail = FALSE)

    Definition 165 Rejection Region.

    🔗
    🔗
    Test Critical value in R Reject when
    Left-tailed qnorm(alpha, 0, 1) \(z\leq z^*\)
    Right-tailed qnorm(alpha, 0, 1, lower.tail = F) \(z\geq z^*\)
    Two-tailed qnorm(alpha / 2, 0, 1, lower.tail = F) \(z \geq +z^*\) or \(z \leq -z^*\)
    For example, at \(\alpha=0.05\text{,}\) the left-tail cutoff is approximately \(-1.645\text{,}\) the right-tail cutoff is approximately 1.645, and the two-tail cutoffs are approximately \(\pm1.960\text{.}\) Draw the curve centered at zero and shade the tail or tails beyond the appropriate cutoff.
    🔗

    Subsection Hypothesis Tests in Context

    Example 166 A Historical Treatment Study: Left-Tailed Test.

    Until recently the death rate from a highly fatal viral infection of the brain, herpes simplex virus encephalitis, has been 70%. A study is run to test the new drug vidarabine for use in treating this disease. Of 50 subjects tested, 14 died. Is this sufficient evidence to support that vidarabine is effective with a 0.01 significance level?

    Example 168 Proportional Mortality: Two-Tailed Test.

    The safety of those who work at or live close to nuclear power plants has been the subject of widely publicized debate in recent years. One possible health hazard due to radiation exposure is an excess of cancer deaths among those exposed. One problem with studying this question is that the number of deaths attributable to either cancer in general or specific types of cancer is small, and reaching statistically significant conclusions is difficult, except after long periods of follow-up. An alternative approach is to perform a proportional-mortality study, whereby the proportion of deaths attributed to a specific cause in an exposed group is compared with the corresponding proportion in a large population. Suppose, for example, that 56 deaths have occurred among 55 to 64 year old male workers in a nuclear power plant and that in 13 of them the cause of death was cancer. Assume, based on vital-statistics reports, that approximately 20\% of all deaths can be attributed to some form of cancer. Is this result significant? Use a significance level of 0.05.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection The Relationship Between Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Tests

    A confidence interval and a hypothesis test both describe compatibility between sample data and a population model. When an interval is obtained by inverting a particular test, values rejected by that test lie outside the corresponding interval.
    🔗
