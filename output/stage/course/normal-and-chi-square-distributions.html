    Subsection An Introduction to the Chi-Square Distribution

    Definition 103 Chi-Square Distribution.

    🔗
    🔗
    One connection to the normal distribution is especially useful: if \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_\nu\) are independent standard normal random variables, then
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} Z_1^2+\cdots+Z_\nu^2\sim\chi^2_{\nu}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Squaring and adding explains why chi-square values cannot be negative. For this construction, the degrees of freedom count the independent squared standard normal components. In statistical applications, the formula for degrees of freedom depends on the procedure.
    🔗
    1. Possible values are nonnegative, and the total area under the curve is 1.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The distribution is right-skewed. As the degrees of freedom increase, its relative skewness decreases and the curve becomes more nearly bell-shaped.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. It is not symmetric for any finite degrees of freedom. It does not have negative values, even when its shape is close to normal.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Its mean is \(\nu\text{,}\) its variance is \(2\nu\text{,}\) and its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{2\nu}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Chi-square models will appear again when we compare observed and expected categorical counts, including goodness-of-fit, independence, and homogeneity tests. For now, focus on the distribution and areas under its curve.
    🔗

    Subsubsection R-Commands for \(\chi^2\) Distribution

    The R functions follow the same pattern as the normal functions. Use pchisq for probabilities and qchisq for cutoffs. Supply degrees of freedom using df.

    Remark 107 Continuous Does Not Mean Normal.

    Normal and chi-square distributions are both continuous, so probabilities are areas and individual points have probability zero. Their shapes differ: a normal curve is symmetric and determined by a mean and standard deviation; a chi-square curve is right-skewed and determined by its degrees of freedom.
