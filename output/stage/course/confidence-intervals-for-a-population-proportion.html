    Subsection What Does the Confidence Level Mean?

    Definition 139. Confidence Level.

    🔗
    🔗
    Imagine taking a random sample, calculating an interval, and repeating this process many times. The sample proportion and interval endpoints change from sample to sample. The population proportion stays fixed. Some of the intervals capture it, and some miss it.
    🔗
    
    A vertical line marks the fixed population proportion \(p\text{.}\) Twenty horizontal confidence intervals are arranged in rows numbered 1 through 20. Seventeen blue intervals cross the vertical line and contain \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Three red intervals miss \(p\text{:}\) intervals 4 and 17 lie entirely to the right of the vertical line, and interval 11 lies entirely to the left.
    🔗
    Circles on the blue intervals and X-shaped markers on the red intervals mark the sample proportions, \(\hat{p}\text{.}\) The sample proportions and interval endpoints vary from sample to sample, while the population proportion stays fixed.
    🔗

    Remark 140. Interpreting an Interval in Context.

    A conventional interpretation is: “We are 95% confident that the true proportion of [the population] who or which [have the characteristic] is between [lower limit] and [upper limit].”
    🔗
    The confidence comes from the repeated-sampling performance of the method. Once an interval has been calculated, it either contains the fixed value of \(p\) or it does not. The 95% is not a probability assigned to \(p\) moving into that particular interval.
    🔗
    A 95% confidence interval also does not say that 95% of individuals have the characteristic, that 95% of sample proportions fall inside this particular interval, or that the next sample result is guaranteed to lie inside it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection The One-Proportion Z-Interval

    In the sampling-distribution model, the standard deviation of \(\hat{p}\) is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\text{.}\) When estimating \(p\text{,}\) we do not know its value. We therefore estimate this standard error using the observed sample proportion.
    🔗

    Definition 141. Estimated Standard Error of a Sample Proportion.

    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 142. Requirements for a One-Proportion Z-Interval.

    1. Binary outcome: Each observation is classified as having or not having the characteristic being counted. Define a success before counting.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Appropriate sampling: The data should come from a random sample of the population of interest or from an appropriate independent binomial model. Consider how the observations were obtained and which population the conclusion can describe.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Independence: The observations should be independent, or approximately so. For a simple random sample without replacement from a finite population of size \(N\text{,}\) use the 10% guideline \(n\leq0.10N\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Enough observed successes and failures: Check both
      🔗
      \begin{gather*} n\hat{p}=x\geq10\qquad\text{and}\qquad n(1-\hat{p})=n-x\geq10. \end{gather*}
      🔗
      For this interval, we check the observed counts because \(p\) is unknown. A large total sample size alone is insufficient if one category has very few observations. These conditions are a practical guideline; the normal interval still has approximate coverage.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Formula 146. One-Proportion Z-Interval.

    Component Formula
    Point estimate \(\hat{p}=x/n\)
    Estimated standard error \(SE=\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\)
    Critical value \(z^*\)
    Margin of error \(E=z^*\cdot SE\)
    Confidence interval \((\hat{p}-E,\hat{p}+E)\)
    Equivalently,
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \hat{p}\pm z^*\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    This normal-approximation interval is also called the Wald interval.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 152. What Changes the Margin of Error?

    1. Confidence level: Holding the data fixed, higher confidence increases the margin of error.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Sample size: Holding the confidence level and sample proportion fixed, larger samples decrease the margin of error. Multiplying \(n\) by 4 cuts it in half.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Sample proportion: Holding the confidence level and sample size fixed, the margin of error is largest when \(\hat{p}=0.50\text{,}\) because \(\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\) is largest there.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The actual interval from a new sample can also shift because its sample proportion can change. A narrow interval is useful only when the sampling and measurement methods are appropriate: the stated margin of error does not account for selection bias, nonresponse bias, or systematic measurement errors.
    🔗

    Subsection One-Sided Confidence Bounds

    Definition 153. Lower and Upper Confidence Bounds.

    A lower confidence bound gives a lower limit for the population parameter. An upper confidence bound gives an upper limit.
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} L=\hat{p}-z^* \cdot SE\qquad\text{(lower bound)},\\ U=\hat{p}+z^* \cdot SE\qquad\text{(upper bound)}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Because a proportion lies between 0 and 1, the corresponding one-sided intervals are \([L,1]\) and \([0,U]\) when the calculated bounds are within that range. The same normal-interval conditions must be checked.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 154. Do Not Split the Tail Area for a One-Sided Bound.

    For a two-sided 90% interval, the 10% outside area is divided into two tails, and \(z^*=\operatorname{qnorm}(0.95)\approx1.645\text{.}\) For a one-sided 90% bound, all of that outside area is in one tail, and \(z^*=\operatorname{qnorm}(0.90)\approx1.282\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Choose whether the question requires a lower bound, upper bound, or two-sided interval before interpreting the result.
    🔗
    🔗
